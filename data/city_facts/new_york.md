# New York

## Orientation and layout

New York City is five boroughs - Manhattan, Brooklyn, Queens, the Bronx and
Staten Island - holding about 8.3 million people. Above 14th Street, Manhattan
follows the 1811 grid: numbered streets run east-west, avenues run north-south,
and Fifth Avenue divides east addresses from west. Below 14th Street the older
street plan takes over, which is why the West Village disorients people who
thought they had the grid figured out. Twenty north-south blocks make roughly one
mile; cross-town blocks are longer. "Uptown" means north, "downtown" means south,
and both double as subway directions. Queens is the largest borough by area and
Brooklyn the largest by population - each would be a major city on its own.

## Neighbourhoods worth your time

The West Village rewards aimless walking: low brownstones, crooked streets, Hudson
River sunsets. The Lower East Side keeps its immigrant history alongside a dense
bar scene. In Brooklyn, Williamsburg is the polished version and Bushwick the
warehouse-and-mural version, while Brooklyn Heights has the Promenade view of the
skyline. Queens is the most linguistically diverse place in the world: Astoria for
Greek and Egyptian food, Jackson Heights for South Asian and Himalayan, Flushing
for a Chinatown that outclasses Manhattan's. Harlem carries the architecture and
music history of the Renaissance. DUMBO packs cobblestones and the Manhattan
Bridge view into a few blocks.

## Food and drink

The defining foods are cheap and specific: a bagel with cream cheese and lox, a
folded 99-cent-era pizza slice, a pastrami sandwich at a Jewish deli such as
Katz's, and a chicken-and-rice platter from a halal street cart. Beyond that,
almost every cuisine on earth is represented at a high level, and the best value
is usually outside Manhattan. Food halls concentrate options - Chelsea Market,
Time Out Market in DUMBO - and Smorgasburg runs outdoors on weekends from spring
to autumn. Brunch is a weekend institution with queues to match. Reservations at
popular restaurants open 30 days ahead and disappear in minutes. Bodegas make
egg-and-cheese sandwiches at any hour.

## Getting around

The subway runs 24 hours a day, seven days a week - the only system of its size
that does - and a single ride is a flat fare regardless of distance. Pay by
tapping a contactless card or phone at the OMNY reader; the fare caps
automatically after twelve rides in a week. The critical distinction is express
versus local: express trains skip most stops, so check the strip map before
boarding. Weekend service changes are constant and posted at station entrances.
Buses are slower but useful cross-town. Citi Bike covers most of Manhattan and
inner Brooklyn. The Staten Island Ferry is free and passes the Statue of Liberty.
From JFK, the AirTrain connects to the subway or the LIRR; Newark has its own
AirTrain to NJ Transit.

## Museums and landmarks

The Metropolitan Museum of Art needs a full day and is pay-what-you-wish only for
New York State residents; everyone else pays a fixed admission. MoMA holds the
canonical modern collection. The American Museum of Natural History added the
Gilder Center in 2023. The Whitney anchors the south end of the High Line, an
elevated park built on a disused freight rail viaduct. The 9/11 Memorial and
Museum sit in the footprints of the towers. Statue of Liberty and Ellis Island
ferries leave from Battery Park and require advance booking for crown access.
Central Park covers 843 acres, and its northern half is quieter than the tourist
loop around the reservoir.

## Seasonality and weather

The climate is humid continental with wide swings. July and August average highs
near 29 to 30 C with humidity that makes the subway platforms genuinely
punishing. January highs sit around 4 C, and nor'easters can drop heavy snow in
January and February, though winters have trended milder. Autumn - late September
through early November - is the best season: dry, clear, 15 to 22 C, with foliage
in the parks by late October. Spring is short and unreliable, swinging between 8
and 24 C in the same week. Hurricane season peaks in September and rarely lands a
direct hit but does bring flooding rain. Summer humidity is what most visitors
underestimate.

## Etiquette and local customs

Tipping is not optional: 18 to 20 percent at restaurants, one to two dollars per
drink at a bar, and a few dollars for a taxi. Sales tax of 8.875 percent is added
at the register, so the shelf price is never the final price. Walk fast, and if
you need to stop, step out of the pedestrian flow first - blocking the sidewalk or
standing on the left of a subway escalator is the local definition of rude. Let
passengers off the train before boarding. New Yorkers are direct rather than
unfriendly; asking for directions usually gets a helpful, brisk answer. Jaywalking
is normal and, since 2024, legal.

## Money, safety and practicalities

The US dollar is effectively cashless in the city - cards and phones work almost
everywhere, and many restaurants have stopped taking cash. Tap water is safe and
comes from the Catskill watershed. Crime is concentrated and statistically low
against visitors; ordinary urban awareness late at night on empty platforms is the
sensible standard. Public restrooms are scarce, so use those in museums, hotel
lobbies and department stores. Broadway tickets are cheapest through the TKTS
booths or the digital lotteries that most shows run. Electrical outlets are 120 V
Type A/B. Emergency services are on 911, and 311 handles everything non-urgent.

## Day trips

The Hudson Valley is the classic escape: Beacon for Dia:Beacon's minimalist art in
a converted factory, 80 minutes on Metro-North, or Cold Spring for hiking Breakneck
Ridge straight from the platform. Storm King Art Center spreads large-scale
sculpture across 500 acres. The Rockaways and Long Beach are reachable by subway
and LIRR respectively for a summer beach day. Philadelphia is 90 minutes by Amtrak
and Washington DC around three hours. New Haven, at 100 minutes on Metro-North,
offers Yale's museums, which are free. Governors Island, a short ferry from Lower
Manhattan, is car-free and open seasonally.
