# Tokyo

## Orientation and layout

Tokyo is not one centre but a chain of them strung along the JR Yamanote loop
line: Tokyo Station, Shinjuku, Shibuya, Ikebukuro and Ueno each function as a
downtown in their own right. Administratively the core is 23 special wards, each
with its own government, inside a metropolis of roughly 14 million people - about
37 million across the greater metropolitan area, the largest in the world.
Addresses are block-based rather than street-based: a location is ward, district,
block, then building number, which is why everyone navigates by station exit and
map pin. Learning your exit number matters more than learning street names;
Shinjuku Station alone has over 200 exits and is the busiest station on earth.

## Neighbourhoods worth your time

Shinjuku pairs the neon of Kabukicho with the calm of Shinjuku Gyoen and the
lantern-lit alleys of Omoide Yokocho. Shibuya is the scramble crossing, youth
fashion and nightlife. Ginza is the luxury grid, with weekend afternoons when the
main street closes to cars. Asakusa keeps the old-town feeling around Senso-ji,
the city's oldest temple. Yanaka survived the wartime bombing and reads like
pre-war Tokyo, all wooden houses and cemetery cherry trees. Shimokitazawa and
Koenji are the second-hand and live-music districts. Nakameguro follows a canal
lined with cherry trees, and Daikanyama next door is quieter and design-led.
Akihabara remains the electronics and anime quarter.

## Food and drink

Tokyo holds more Michelin stars than any other city, but the everyday food is the
real story: a bowl of ramen for under 1,000 yen, standing soba at a station
counter eaten in five minutes, conveyor sushi, and department-store basements -
depachika - that are among the best food halls anywhere. Izakaya are the default
evening venue, ordering several small dishes with beer or highballs. Specialist
shops go deep rather than wide, so a tempura restaurant serves tempura and
nothing else. Convenience stores are genuinely good for onigiri, egg sandwiches
and coffee. Toyosu Market replaced Tsukiji's inner wholesale market in 2018, but
the Tsukiji outer market still trades in prepared food and knives.

## Getting around

Rail does almost everything. JR East runs the Yamanote loop and the regional
lines; Tokyo Metro and Toei run the subways, and they are separate companies, so
a transfer between them can cost extra. Get a Suica or Pasmo IC card - or the
version in your phone wallet - and tap through everything including convenience
stores. Trains are punctual to the minute and stop around midnight, which shapes
the night: miss the last train and you are in a taxi or a karaoke booth until
05:00. From Narita, the Narita Express takes about an hour to Tokyo Station; from
Haneda, the monorail reaches Hamamatsucho in around 15 minutes. Rush hour on the
Chuo and Saikyo lines is as compressed as the videos suggest.

## Temples, gardens and modern landmarks

Senso-ji in Asakusa dates to 645 and is approached through the Kaminarimon gate
and the Nakamise shopping street. Meiji Jingu sits inside a planted forest of
100,000 donated trees beside Harajuku and is the calmest place in central Tokyo.
The Imperial Palace East Gardens occupy the old castle keep and are free. Tokyo
Skytree stands 634 metres, the tallest tower in the world, while Tokyo Tower at
333 metres is the 1958 landmark it replaced. Shibuya Sky and the Tokyo
Metropolitan Government Building in Shinjuku both give high views, the second one
free. teamLab's digital art venues book out weeks ahead. Ueno Park concentrates
the national museums in one walkable green space.

## Seasonality and weather

Tokyo has a humid subtropical climate with four sharply distinct seasons. Cherry
blossom peaks in the last week of March or the first week of April and lasts
barely ten days. Tsuyu, the rainy season, runs from early June to mid-July.
August is the hard month: highs near 31 C with humidity that makes it feel
hotter, and it is when the city empties for Obon. September and October bring
typhoon risk, occasionally shutting the trains for a day. Late October to early
December is arguably the best time - dry, clear, with ginkgo and maple colour
peaking in late November. Winter is cold but sunny, with January highs around
10 C and snow that falls perhaps once or twice a year.

## Etiquette and local customs

Do not tip - it is not expected anywhere, and pressing money on staff creates
awkwardness rather than gratitude. Stand on the left of escalators in Tokyo, the
right in Osaka. Eating while walking is generally avoided; people eat beside the
shop they bought from. Keep phone calls off trains and phones on silent, called
manner mode. Public bins are scarce, a legacy of the 1995 subway attack, so carry
rubbish until you find one at a convenience store. Shoes come off at the entrance
to homes, ryokan, temples and some restaurants - watch for the raised floor.
Slurping noodles is fine. Many onsen still refuse visible tattoos, though some
now allow them if covered.

## Money and practicalities

Japan is far more card-friendly than a decade ago, but cash still matters at
small restaurants, shrines and markets - carry a few thousand yen. Foreign cards
work reliably at 7-Eleven and Japan Post ATMs, less so at bank machines.
Consumption tax is 10 percent and is usually shown in the price, with tax-free
shopping available to tourists at larger stores on presentation of a passport.
Free wi-fi is patchy, so a travel eSIM or pocket wi-fi is worth the cost. The
Japan Rail Pass only pays off if you are taking multiple shinkansen trips; for a
Tokyo-only stay it does not. Coin lockers at stations solve the luggage problem
between checkout and departure.

## Day trips

Kamakura is under an hour from Tokyo Station and combines the Great Buddha, the
Hase-dera temple and a beach. Nikko, two hours north, holds the elaborate
Toshogu shrine complex and mountain scenery, and is a UNESCO World Heritage site.
Hakone is the classic onsen circuit of ropeway, pirate boat and volcanic valley,
with Mount Fuji views when the weather cooperates. Kawaguchiko, on the Fuji Five
Lakes, gives the closest reliable views of the mountain. Yokohama is 30 minutes
away and worth it for the waterfront and the largest Chinatown in Japan. Kyoto is
reachable in 2 hours 15 minutes by shinkansen, though it deserves longer than a
day.
