# Paris

## Orientation and layout

Paris is divided into 20 arrondissements that spiral clockwise from the 1st,
which holds the Louvre and the Tuileries, out to the 20th in the east. The Seine
splits the city into the Right Bank (north, larger, more commercial) and the Left
Bank (south, home to the Latin Quarter and Saint-Germain). The city proper is
compact - roughly 105 square kilometres, about 10 kilometres across - so walking
between neighbouring districts is normal and often faster than transferring on
the Metro. Addresses carry the arrondissement in the postcode: 75004 is the 4th,
75018 the 18th. The two islands in the river, Ile de la Cite and Ile Saint-Louis,
form the historic centre and are where Notre-Dame stands.

## Neighbourhoods worth your time

Le Marais (3rd and 4th) is the medieval quarter that survived Haussmann's
rebuilding: narrow streets, the Place des Vosges, independent boutiques, and the
historic Jewish quarter along Rue des Rosiers. Saint-Germain-des-Pres (6th) is
the literary Left Bank of Sartre and de Beauvoir, now expensive and elegant.
Montmartre (18th) sits on the city's highest hill under Sacre-Coeur and is
genuinely steep - use the funicular. Canal Saint-Martin (10th) draws a younger
crowd to its iron footbridges and canalside picnics. Belleville (20th) is the
most diverse district, with strong Chinese and North African communities and a
serious street-art scene. Batignolles (17th) is the quiet, residential option.

## Food and drink

The daily rhythm is a boulangerie in the morning, a long lunch, and dinner from
19:30 at the earliest - kitchens in many bistros close between 14:30 and 19:00.
Market streets are the best value: Rue Montorgueil (2nd), Rue Mouffetard (5th),
and the Marche d'Aligre (12th), which combines a covered hall with an open-air
market. Classic bistro dishes are steak frites, confit de canard, blanquette de
veau and onion soup. The natural-wine bars of the 11th arrondissement changed how
the city drinks. A carafe d'eau - free tap water - is your right in any
restaurant, and asking for it is not rude. Falafel on Rue des Rosiers and Breton
crepes near Montparnasse are the reliable cheap meals.

## Getting around

The Metro has 16 lines and a station roughly every 500 metres; it runs from about
05:30 to 01:15, an hour later on Fridays, Saturdays and holiday eves. The RER is
the faster regional network - RER B connects Charles de Gaulle airport to the
centre in about 35 minutes, RER C serves Versailles. Paper tickets have been
phased out in favour of the Navigo Easy card and phone ticketing; a Navigo
Decouverte weekly pass is the cheapest option for a stay of several days. Buses
are slower but give you the city above ground. Velib bike share has docking
stations everywhere, and the network of protected bike lanes expanded sharply
after 2020. Taxis and ride-hailing are plentiful but slow in central traffic.

## Museums and landmarks

The Louvre is the largest art museum in the world and closes on Tuesdays; book a
timed slot and enter through the Carrousel or Richelieu entrances to skip the
pyramid queue. The Musee d'Orsay, in a converted railway station, holds the
Impressionist collection and closes Mondays. The Centre Pompidou covers modern
and contemporary art behind its exposed-pipework facade. Sainte-Chapelle's upper
chapel is fifteen metres of 13th-century stained glass and is best on a bright
morning. Notre-Dame reopened in December 2024 after the 2019 fire and its
restoration. The Musee Rodin has a garden worth the ticket on its own. Many
national museums are free on the first Sunday of the month.

## Seasonality and weather

Paris has a temperate oceanic climate: mild, changeable, and wetter than its
reputation suggests, with rain spread thinly across the year rather than
concentrated in a season. July and August average highs near 25 C, with heatwaves
now regularly pushing past 35 C in buildings that mostly lack air conditioning.
January highs sit around 7 C and frost is common but heavy snow is rare. Late
April to June and September to mid-October are the best windows: long daylight,
moderate crowds, comfortable temperatures. Many family-run restaurants and shops
close for two to four weeks in August when Parisians leave for the coast, so
check before travelling far for a specific address.

## Etiquette and local customs

Say "Bonjour" when you enter any shop, bakery or restaurant, and "Au revoir" when
you leave - skipping it is the single most common way visitors seem rude without
meaning to. Use "Madame" or "Monsieur" rather than starting cold. Service is
included by law, so tipping is rounding up or leaving a euro or two for good
service, not a percentage. Keep your voice down on the Metro and in restaurants;
the ambient volume is noticeably lower than in North America. Attempting a few
words of French before switching to English is received far better than opening
in English. Dress tends to be understated - dark, plain, well-fitted.

## Money, safety and practicalities

The currency is the euro and contactless cards work almost everywhere, including
on buses and in bakeries, though a few small bistros still have a card minimum.
Tap water is safe. Pickpocketing is the realistic risk, concentrated at Chatelet
and Gare du Nord, on Line 1, around Sacre-Coeur, and at the Eiffel Tower - keep
bags zipped and in front of you in crowds. Pharmacies with a green cross handle
minor medical problems and the emergency number is 112. Sunday closures still
apply to many shops outside designated tourist zones like the Marais and the
Champs-Elysees. Public toilets are the free grey sanisettes on the street.

## Day trips

Versailles is 45 minutes on RER C to Versailles Chateau Rive Gauche; the palace
closes Mondays and the gardens are the point - go early or stay late. Giverny,
Monet's garden, runs April to November and takes about 90 minutes by train to
Vernon plus a shuttle. Fontainebleau is the quieter royal palace, less than an
hour by train from Gare de Lyon, with a forest that draws boulderers from across
Europe. Chantilly combines a chateau, the Conde art collection, and the stables.
Reims, in Champagne, is 45 minutes on the TGV from Gare de l'Est, which makes a
cellar tour a plausible day out rather than an overnight trip.
